In the annals of technology history, few devices command the same respect as the Commodore PET. Released in 1977, it is widely credited as the world's first mass-produced personal computer. While other machines like the Apple II and TRS-80 were vying for attention in the same year, the PET established a fundamental blueprint for what a personal computer should be.

**A Legacy of Innovation**
The Commodore PET wasn't just a novelty; it was a serious business tool. Its initial release focused heavily on the educational and industrial markets. With a chassis that resembled a small office typewriter and a built-in keyboard, it bridged the gap between typewriters and electronic data processing.

**Key Specifications and Features**
Under the hood, the PET was a powerhouse for its time. Key specifications included:
*   **Processor:** MOS Technology 6502 processor.
*   **Memory:** A standard 4KB of RAM (expandable).
*   **Storage:** Used cassette tapes as the primary storage medium.
*   **Display:** The early models featured a membrane keyboard and a light-emitting diode (LED) multi-line display, later models moving to integrated screens.
*   **OS:** It shipped with Commodore BASIC, allowing users to write and run their own code immediately.

**The "Beige Brick" Aesthetic**
The design of the PET was utilitarian. It came encased in a beige, plastic housing that protected the internals. This "brick" design made it durable and easy to transport, contributing to its popularity in schools and universities where robustness was key.

**Impact on Modern Computing**
The success of the PET laid the groundwork for Commodore's future empire. It proved that there was a viable market for affordable computing. The lessons learned from the PET's input/output interfaces and basic architecture were integral to the development of the legendary Commodore 64 and the ultra-modern Amiga line.

Today, the Commodore PET remains a beloved icon among vintage computing enthusiasts and historians alike. Its legacy lives on in the devices we carry in our pockets every day.
