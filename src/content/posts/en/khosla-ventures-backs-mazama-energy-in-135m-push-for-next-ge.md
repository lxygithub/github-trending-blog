A major new investment is heating up the race to make advanced geothermal power a commercial reality. Mazama Energy, a startup backed by the visionary VC firm Khosla Ventures, has just announced a $135 million funding round to accelerate its mission of drilling deep to tap the Earth's most potent heat.

The core of Mazama's approach is targeting "superhot rock" located roughly three miles (or about 5 kilometers) underground. At these depths, temperatures are extreme—often exceeding 400°C (752°F). Drilling into this environment creates a unique opportunity: when water is injected, it flashes into high-pressure, high-temperature steam, which can then be used to drive turbines with exceptional efficiency.

The promise of this technology is transformative. While conventional geothermal plants are geographically limited to areas with naturally occurring hydrothermal systems, superhot-rock geothermal has the potential to work almost anywhere on the planet. A single well, in the right conditions, is capable of generating a staggering 15 megawatts (MW) of electricity 24/7. That's enough to power over 10,000 homes consistently, day and night, regardless of weather or time of day.

This funding round will be crucial for Mazama to move from theory to practice. The capital will support the drilling of multiple test wells, allowing the company to refine its techniques for surviving and operating in such harsh subterranean conditions. The engineering challenges are immense, from developing drill bits that can withstand the heat to ensuring wellbore stability.

The implications for the clean energy transition are profound. Geothermal power offers the "holy grail" of renewable energy: **baseload power**. Unlike solar and wind, it doesn't depend on the sun shining or the wind blowing. If Mazama and companies like it can successfully commercialize superhot-rock geothermal, it could unlock a vast, new source of always-on, zero-carbon energy to complement intermittent renewables and displace fossil fuel power plants.

Khosla Ventures' backing is a significant vote of confidence, aligning with their history of funding disruptive technologies that can have a massive impact on climate change. This $135 million investment isn't just about a single company; it's about funding the future of the world's energy grid—one superhot well at a time. The coming years of drilling and testing will determine just how close we are to unlocking this immense subterranean power source.
